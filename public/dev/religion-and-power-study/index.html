The question

What happens when religious identity stops being a way of following Jesus and starts being a way of belonging, ruling, or excluding?

Jesus’s disputes with Pharisees are not a license to condemn Judaism, Jewish people, or every religious authority. They are particular first-century arguments preserved inside a Jewish religious world. The Book of Mormon offers a separate set of narratives where status, priestcraft, political power, pride, persecution, and repentance can be traced through different people, groups, and events.

The goal is not a partisan scorecard. The goal is to let a structured corpus surface recurring religious patterns, then mark the step from data to observation to modern interpretation.

A NOTE ON HISTORY

The Pharisees are not a modern type.

Pharisees were a diverse Jewish movement. Jesus and His earliest disciples were Jewish, and the Gospel disputes happened within first-century Judaism. Here, “Pharisee” names the people and events represented in specific Gospel narratives; it is never used as a synonym for Jewish hypocrisy.

Modern application, carefully bounded

Warning signs are questions for discipleship—not labels for neighbors.

“Christian nationalism” is not synonymous with Christianity. It is also not a synonym for all conservative Christians, Republicans, Trump voters, or supporters of a particular policy. Immigration enforcement, taxation, criminal justice, national defense, elections, and similar questions can involve legitimate disagreement.

These prompts ask about recurring structures: identity, power, exclusion, selective law, pride, and hostility. They do not tell anyone how to vote, and they do not turn a policy position into proof that a person rejects Christ.

The limit of the graph

The corpus can establish connections. It cannot read a modern heart.

That is why this study separates data, observation, and interpretation. The strongest use of a knowledge graph is not to manufacture certainty. It is to make the evidence traceable enough that a reader can disagree with an interpretation without losing sight of the underlying text.
